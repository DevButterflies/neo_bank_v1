Provenance, scope and how to read this document

This reference is a snapshot of the engine as implemented in the repository at the build date on the cover. It is deliberately opinionated about one distinction that the rest of the corpus blurs: what the bank is designed to do versus what the shipped runtime actually does. Every chapter states the design intent first, then the verified behaviour, then the gap.

Part I

The System

Before the pillars, three things must be understood: what business the engine is actually in, which layer outranks every other layer, and how one Python process presents itself as five services without losing the ability to restart cleanly.

Chapter 1

System purpose and operating model

The engine is a compact, deterministic implementation of a small-business lender that funds loans through a layered capital stack and protects its balance sheet with a pooled guarantee. It is not a generic core-banking product. Almost every design decision follows from two facts: the borrower is an MSME with thin data, and the lender does not take deposits.

1.1 What the engine is

The shipped system is a single FastAPI application that mounts four pillar sub-applications plus a governance sub-application, exposes a French-PCG balance sheet, and serves a demo single-page application. All of it runs in one process against one SQLite file. The composition root is orchestrator/main.py; the fixed mount table is at orchestrator/main.py:44-49 and the runtime wiring at orchestrator/main.py:114-123.

The engine calls itself a “bank system”, but its business model is narrower than a deposit bank’s. It originates MSME loans, funds them from a six-position capital stack, and holds a pooled guarantee that absorbs crystallised losses. There is no deposit book, no payment rail, and no retail current account. The absence of deposits is not an omission; it is the defining constraint of the guarantee-intermediation model, and it is why the liquidity reserve is a funding-side buffer rather than a depositor-run buffer (p4/domain/buffer_account.py:152-174).

1.2 The five-pillar division of labour

The corpus describes five pillars. Four are implemented here. The fifth — pledge and NFT submission — appears only as a boundary: an inbound contract and a firewall. The Orchestrator is not a sixth pillar; it is the layer above all pillars and it prevails over them without exception.

The division is not decorative. It explains why Pillar 3 contains no pricing formula, why Pillar 4 contains no borrower record, and why the staging signal from Pillar 2 bypasses Pillar 3 entirely. It also explains the single most important negative rule in the system: Pillar 4 has no per-loan interface with Pillar 3. Pillar 3 only ever sees a confirmed packet or no packet (p4/integration/pillar4.py:7-16; p3/interfaces/non_interfaces.py).

1.3 The micro/macro cut

The organising principle behind the whole architecture is a cut between events and meaning. Pillar 3 is the micro layer: it records what happened to a loan. Pillar 4 is the macro layer: it aggregates those events across the book and decides what they mean for capital, liquidity, provisioning, and burden-sharing. Pillar 3 is explicitly forbidden from computing any allocation.

Micro — Pillar 3Macro — Pillar 4
Per-loan events: repayment, fee, risk premium, default, recovery.Aggregation across the book.
No allocation logic.All allocation, burden-sharing, repartition, provisioning, and capital-impact computation.
Records what happened.Decides what the recorded facts mean.
Never sees a prudential veto event.Expresses a veto only by refusing to confirm a Pillar 2 packet.
Why this cut exists

If Pillar 3 computed allocations, every new prudential rule would require a change to the execution layer, and the execution layer would slowly become a second policy engine. The cut keeps Pillar 3 dumb on purpose. The engine enforces it with an allocation guard that inspects postings for allocation-like fields (p3/governance/allocation_guard.py).

1.4 Four events, never interchangeable

The corpus locks four economically different events into separate lexical and code paths: release, redemption, foreclosure, and call. They must not share a vocabulary, a posting type, or a code path. In the implementation the rule is enforced structurally: the Pillar 4 event enum has no redemption member at all, and each of the other three has its own module and its own service (p4/domain/events.py:74-83).

This is stronger than a naming convention: a developer cannot accidentally route a release through a call path because the types do not share a member.

1.5 Two guarantee layers

The engine keeps two collateral concepts structurally separate, and the separation is enforced by distinct Python types rather than a boolean flag:

The corpus calls this the Two-Guarantee-Layer Rule. Conflating the two is the structural error the rule exists to prevent: a facility pledge is not pool collateral, and pool collateral is not security for a specific loan. Because they are separate classes, an accidental substitution is a type error, not a business-logic bug.

1.6 Conventional and Adaptive modes

The whole bank has two governance-controlled operating modes, defined by the SI-1 instrument. The mode is not a build phase and not a feature flag in the ordinary sense; it is a governance state that changes which rules are armed.

Conventional Mode — active

Static tier structure, classical foreclosure, fixed veto thresholds, no adaptive scoring. A complete, licensable, standalone bank.

Current stage: 0/1. Conventional Mode is the permanent floor.

Adaptive Mode — dormant

Conventional Mode plus CASS scoring, ABSF burden-sharing, the Reverse Restoration Waterfall, and Tier B-Fees interruption/resumption.

Gated behind SI-1 Gate G1 and the unresolved CASS feed specification.

In the code the adaptive components exist, compile, and are structurally complete. They are not instantiated by the runtime, and they refuse to run unless the mode gate is switched. The CASS engine, for example, calls require_adaptive() before doing any work (p4/adaptive_dormant/cass_engine.py:82-97). This is the corpus’s “deactivation is not deletion” rule made real: the machinery is present, but the switch is governance-owned.

The single hinge

Adaptive Mode cannot be activated by code alone. The full Pillar 2 + Pillar 4 CASS data-feed specification is a named PENDING item, and the weights and zone thresholds are unresolved. Until governance ratifies them, the adaptive layer stops with a named exception rather than guessing (p4/domain/pending.py:50-57).

1.7 Deployment topology and the restart contract

The production composition mounts the pillar apps with the orchestrator’s database path, so Pillar 3 and Pillar 4 share data/orchestrator.db with governance and the demo state (orchestrator/main.py:114-123). Running a pillar module directly (uvicorn bank.pillars.api.p4:app) uses its own configured SQLite path instead (core/config.py:25-30; p4/api.py:849). This dual-mode behaviour is intentional but easy to miss, and it is the source of a documentation contradiction noted in Chapter 10.

The restart contract is explicit. GET /ready opens a fresh Pillar 3 and Pillar 4 runtime over the same store, serialises each snapshot, and compares it to the persisted blob. If either rehydrated snapshot differs, it returns HTTP 503 with a per-pillar result (orchestrator/main.py:229-247). This is a stronger promise than “the service starts”: it asserts that the in-memory engine and the stored engine are byte-identical after a reload.

The restart contract has a blind spot

Governance is mounted as a sub-application, and Starlette does not run a mounted sub-application’s lifespan. As a result the governance app’s current_canon_status is never populated in the composed process, and the canon-enforcement middleware fails on every mutating request. Chapter 2 explains the mechanism and its blast radius.

What to carry into the pillar chapters
  • The Orchestrator outranks every pillar; a pillar cannot carve out an exception.
  • Pillar 3 is deliberately dumb: it records, posts, and reconciles, but never allocates.
  • Pillar 4 expresses a veto only at Pillar 2; Pillar 3 never sees a veto event.
  • The four events are structurally distinct, not just differently named.
  • Conventional Mode is the floor; Adaptive Mode is gated by governance and a PENDING methodology.
  • One process, one store, and a restart contract that compares rehydrated snapshots byte for byte.
Chapter 2 · Orchestrator and governance

The rulebook above the pillars

The Orchestrator owns the definitions that every pillar is forbidden to redefine: the canon in force, the vocabulary, the tier table, the inter-pillar interfaces, the operating mode, and the register of questions that code is not allowed to answer. In the shipped engine this rulebook is a set of relational registries plus a thin enforcement middleware. The registries are rich and faithfully seeded. The enforcement is narrower than the prose suggests, and in the composed process it is partly broken.

2.1 Precedence and the shape of the governance layer

The Orchestrator is Layer 1 of the decision hierarchy. Its canonical content is served by a mounted FastAPI sub-application at /governance, under the API prefix /api/v1, so the effective path is /governance/api/v1/… (orchestrator/main.py:44-49, 114-123; governance/main.py:75-85). The sub-application exposes nine registries:

Canon

Versions, lineage, ratification instruments, current-version pointer.

Lexicon

Five categories and 27 canonical terms, with rename and validation support.

Tiers

Four tier definitions, protection edges, pillar boundaries.

Interfaces

The seven §6.1–6.7 rules and a flow validator.

CASS stub

CIS-1 contract shape and five fields. No methodology.

Staging

SI-1 mode, stage, and gates G0–G3.

Changes

Amendment proposals, dependency reviews, ratification votes.

Veto log

P4 freeze notifications and governance resolution.

PENDING

Nine open governance items with a code-resolution gate.

The governance layer does not make credit, execution, or prudential decisions. It stores the definitions those decisions must conform to, and it logs the exceptions (vetoes and amendments). That division is deliberate: the Orchestrator is a source of truth, not an operational actor.

Verified behaviour

Current-version selection is by the is_current flag, not by the highest version number. Ratifying a version updates the flag on every row in one transaction, so exactly one canon can be current (canon_service.py:64-93). Superseding a version clears the flag without promoting a successor (canon_service.py:96-105).

The HTTP surface supports listing versions and lineage, reading one version with its instruments, creating a version, ratifying it, and superseding it (governance/routers/canon.py:15-72). There is no authentication on any of these routes. Authority is assumed to come from the network boundary, not from the application.

Scope limit

The lexicon is a registry, not a compiler. Nothing in Pillars 1–4 consults it at runtime. A packet can use a forbidden synonym and still be accepted; the violation is only detectable if someone submits the term to /lexicon/validate.

Contradictory edge

The fourth row asserts that Tier A absorbs losses from Tier D. That contradicts the canon’s Tier A posture (par-protected, zero loss absorption, insured) and the first three rows, which place A at the protected end of the stack. It appears to be an attempted inverse rendering of the D→C→B→A chain, but the direction is wrong: if it is an inverse view it should be D ABSORBS_LOSS_FROM A or a separate PROTECTS edge, not A absorbing from D. No engine path reads this table for loss allocation today, so the immediate blast radius is representational. It matters the moment a consumer trusts the registry instead of the code (seed.py:429-435).

Two tier tables, two shapes

Governance seeds four tiers (A–D). Pillar 4’s canonical table has six positions: Tier A, Tier B, Senior Institutional Debt, Tier C, Tier B-Fees, and Tier D. The two non-letter positions have no governance row. This is a genuine representational divergence, not just a summary: a client that reads /governance/api/v1/tiers cannot discover Senior Debt or Tier B-Fees, even though both participate in Pillar 4’s ABSF and bail-in logic. Chapter 7 documents the six-position table.

The tier service also exposes the pillar-boundary registry and a protection-rule listing (tier_service.py:56-78). The sole-locus rule for Tier A insurance status is implemented as an endpoint that always stamps the authority string “Orchestrator Canon §4.6 (sole locus)” (tier_service.py:38-54). The ENFORCE_SOLE_LOCUS flag branches to a literal pass; disabling it changes nothing. Because this is the only writer for the field, the structural outcome is still sole-locus, but the flag is not a control.

Two asymmetries in this table are worth noticing. First, Pillar 4 is the only pillar whose decides list is purely policy-level (“what the admissibility policy is” and “whether a guarantee call is settled and how”); it never decides a case. Second, Pillar 3’s decides list is empty. Execution is modelled as a deterministic function of a confirmed packet plus recorded events, not as an agent with discretion. That is the data-level expression of the “no-allocation-logic” rule.

2.9 Change control, veto log, and the PENDING register

These three registries carry the governance memory that the rest of the engine depends on. Each has a distinct job.

Change control

A change proposal has a type, a target canon version, a proposer, and two flags: whether it requires a dependency review and whether it requires a G→S review (change_control_service.py:31-60). A MIGRATION_G2S proposal forces both flags on. When a dependency review is required, three review rows are auto-created—Pillars 2, 3, and 4—in PENDING status. Ratification is refused while any review is still PENDING (change_control_service.py:122-141). Approval writes a RatificationVote and, if no instrument number is supplied, generates one by counting existing votes (change_control_service.py:143-166).

This is the most procedural registry in the system, and the only one that encodes a real multi-party gate. Its weakness is identity: the proposer, reviewers, and voter are free-text fields, and there is no check that the same actor did not fill all three roles.

Veto log

Every P4 freeze is supposed to produce an Orchestrator notification. The log records a sequential event number, track (1 or 2), scope, optional facility, the P4 signal, the P2 packet action, the notification timestamp, and an OPEN review status (veto_service.py:25-40). Resolution has exactly two outcomes: RESTART_AUTHORIZED, which stamps the authoriser and time, or PACKET_REMAINS_WITHHELD (veto_service.py:63-81). The table is a log, not the live veto state; live state lives in Pillar 4. The two are not synchronised by the governance layer, so a “resolved” log entry does not itself lift a freeze.

2.10 Enforcement and startup: where the rulebook leaks

The governance gate is a single Starlette middleware (governance/main.py:73; middleware/audit.py:17-68). For every non-GET request except the whitelisted documentation and health paths, it reads the current canon status from application state and returns HTTP 409 unless the status is exactly RATIFIED. It also writes one audit log line per request. That is the whole enforcement model.

DEFECT 1 — mounted governance has no canon status

The status is loaded in the governance application’s lifespan (governance/main.py:35-47). Starlette does not run the lifespan of a mounted sub-application. The orchestrator mounts governance at /governance (orchestrator/main.py:165) and its own lifespan never sets current_canon_status; it only creates tables, seeds Tier D, seeds P1 candidates, and starts the auto-tick (orchestrator/main.py:54-100). Consequently, in the composed process, request.app.state.current_canon_status does not exist.

The middleware dereferences it directly (audit.py:38). The result is an AttributeError and HTTP 500 on every mutating governance route—not the intended 409. GET routes are unaffected. Run governance standalone (uvicorn bank.orchestrator.governance.main:app) and the lifespan runs, so the same request returns 409 or succeeds as designed. The behaviour therefore depends on how the app was launched, which is the worst kind of governance inconsistency.

DEFECT 2 — a fresh production boot has no canon

The canonical registries are seeded only from the demo bootstrap: bank.orchestrator.demo.seed imports and calls run_governance_seed() (demo/seed.py:210-213). The orchestrator lifespan does not call it (orchestrator/main.py:54-100), and no production script invokes it. A fresh non-demo database therefore has tables but no canon rows. Reading /canon/current returns 404; if the middleware worked, every mutation would return 409 because the status would be NONE. The system cannot bootstrap its own rulebook through the API: ratifying a canon is itself a mutation, and the gate requires an already ratified canon. Seeding is the only way out, and seeding is demo-only.

DEFECT 3 — the gate checks a string, not a policy

Enforcement is global and coarse. It does not distinguish creating a draft canon from ratifying one, or recording a veto from changing the mode. It does not check the actor, the target, or whether the specific gate conditions for that action are met. The three configuration flags are unevenly implemented: ENFORCE_CANON_RATIFIED is real, ENFORCE_PENDING_DISCIPLINE is real, ENFORCE_SOLE_LOCUS is a no-op branch (config.py:33-35; tier_service.py:47-50). The result is a governance layer that can stop all writes but cannot enforce the specific rules it stores.

What the audit middleware does do

Every request that reaches it gets a UUID, a method/path/status/duration log line, and a request ID in the 409 body. It does not persist an audit record, does not include the actor, and does not log request bodies. The log is operational telemetry, not a governance audit trail.

2.11 What to carry into the pillar chapters

Carry-forward
  • The canon, lexicon, tier table, interface rules, mode, and PENDING register are data, served by governance; the pillars read or reproduce them but do not own them.
  • V3.4 is the ratified canon; the lineage is complete and the current pointer is a flag, not a version sort.
  • The seven interface rules define permitted directions; the field-level contract is not in the registry and is PENDING.
  • CIS-1 is a shape only. No weights, zones, or methodology exist in code.
  • The mode switch records a claim; it does not verify SI-1 gates. Pillar 4’s own gate is the operative control.
  • In the composed process, governance mutations fail with 500 because the mounted lifespan never sets the canon status. Standalone governance behaves as designed.
  • The PENDING discipline is a real hard gate; the sole-locus flag is not.
  • Two registries diverge from the operational model: governance has four tiers while Pillar 4 has six positions, and one protection edge contradicts the Tier A posture.
Chapter 3 · Core, persistence and the bilan

The shared floor: one database, two storage models, and a balance sheet that refuses to lie

Beneath the pillars sit four cross-cutting concerns: configuration, the HTTP application factory, persistence, and the French-PCG balance sheet. The first three are deliberately minimal. The fourth is the most epistemically careful component in the repository: it distinguishes a tautology from a proof, reports prudential buffers as memo items rather than cash, and lowers its own confidence flag when the numbers cannot be independently checked.

3.2 One file, two storage mechanisms

data/orchestrator.db carries two unrelated persistence models side by side:

MechanismWhat lives thereAccess pattern

Governance and P1 share one declarative Base; P1 imports it so init_models() creates the candidate table alongside the governance tables (governance/database.py:31-41; p1/models.py:1-17). The pillar engines do not use SQLAlchemy at all. They use a tiny key/value store whose entire schema is kv(key TEXT PRIMARY KEY, value TEXT NOT NULL) (pillars/api/storage.py:16-30).

Why this matters for reconciliation

The SQL side has constraints, relationships, and per-field updates. The KV side has none: a snapshot is written as one JSON string, so two concurrent writers to the same key can lose an update without any database error. The engine serialises its own access through in-process locks, but the store itself does not provide compare-and-swap or a version column. The single-process deployment is what makes this safe; it is not safe by construction.

3.3 The KV store and the lossless codec

KvStore opens a fresh aiosqlite connection per operation, ensures the table exists, and closes the connection (storage.py:22-56). Writes are a single upsert (INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE), committed immediately. There is no transaction spanning two keys, which is why the bilan’s config write and audit append can diverge (see §3.7).

The engine snapshots are not plain JSON. The codec tags types that JSON would otherwise erase (pillars/api/codec.py:1-20):

{"__enum__": "ClassName", "value": "MEMBER"} {"__dt__": "2026-10-22T12:00:00+00:00"} {"__set__": [...]} {"__tuple__": [...]} {"__dc__": "DataclassName", ...fields...} Persistence markers. decode() resolves them against caller-supplied class and enum registries because the engine dataclasses are spread across modules.

Two codecs exist for two different audiences. encode() is the persistence codec: it preserves markers so a snapshot round-trips losslessly. to_wire() is the API codec: it flattens enums to their values, datetimes to ISO strings, and dataclasses to plain dicts, so no marker ever reaches a client (codec.py:24-68). Conflating them would either leak internal markers or destroy type information on reload; the split is a genuine invariant of the persistence design.

Restart equality is a codec property

The restart proof compares encode(await runtime._snapshot()) with the stored JSON blob. It therefore proves that the codec is deterministic and that the in-memory reconstruction serialises back to the same bytes. It does not prove that every field was semantically validated, and it does not cover keys outside the two snapshots.

3.4 The restart contract

GET /ready is not a liveness probe. It opens a fresh P3Runtime and P4Runtime over the same store, loads both, serialises each snapshot with the persistence codec, and compares the result byte-for-byte with the stored blob (orchestrator/main.py:223-251). Both checks must pass; otherwise the endpoint returns HTTP 503 with a per-pillar result.

This catches a class of bug that ordinary health checks miss: a load path that silently drops a field, coerces an enum, or loses a set will change the re-encoded bytes even though the service is “up.” It is a strong check of persistence fidelity. It is not a check of business correctness.

Coverage gap in the strongest probe

The restart contract covers only p3_state_v1 and p4_state_v1. It does not check the P2 engine snapshot, the governance registries, the P1 candidate table, or the bilan config/audit keys. A corrupted P2 snapshot or a governance table that failed to seed can coexist with a green /ready. The probe proves exactly two things well; operators should not read it as “the whole system survives restart.”

The probe is also not read-only in the strict sense: opening a KvStore runs CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS kv (storage.py:22-27). Against a missing or empty database, a readiness check can create the table. That is benign for the intended file, but it means /ready can leave a trace and is not a pure observer.

3.5 The bilan as a pure read model

GET /bilan is mounted on the orchestrator root, not inside a pillar (orchestrator/main.py:181-186). It re-reads the shared store on every call—no cache and no engine instance—so the answer reflects the last committed P3/P4 write. The store layer opens SQLite in URI read-only mode (file:<path>?mode=ro) and degrades any failure (missing file, missing table, missing row, corrupt blob) to an empty result rather than raising (bilan/store.py:1-70). The balance sheet is therefore always available, even when the database is half-built.

compute_bilan() is pure: configuration in, P3/P4 snapshots in, a payload out, no I/O (bilan/compute.py:1-15, 257-465). It recognises four flow terms from the P3 posting log: disbursement, repayment, interest, and origination fee. Loss events are classified by memo, not by event type, because a single LOSS_RECOVERY posting can carry three economically opposite things: a zero-amount default marker, a write-off (principal that will never return), and a recovery receipt (cash that did return) (compute.py:93-170; p3/domain/memos.py).

3.7 Bilan configuration: the one write path with a key

BilanConfig holds the non-loan assets and third-party liabilities: fixed assets, other receivables, capital reserve, prior result, Senior Institutional Debt, Tier C, other borrowings, customer deposits, other debts, and optional opening cash (bilan/config.py:21-52). It is stored as one JSON blob under bilan_config_v1. Capital social is intentionally absent: Tier D is read live from P4 so a governance capital decision moves both faces.

GET /bilan/config returns the stored values plus the resolved opening cash and a boolean saying whether it was configured or derived (router.py:113-156). That flag exists because a resolved number alone cannot tell a client whether a human set it.

PUT /bilan/config is the only bilan path that writes, and the only endpoint in the whole application with an explicit credential. It requires an X-Admin-Token header matching BANK_ORCH_BILAN_ADMIN_TOKEN using a constant-time comparison. If the environment variable is unset, the endpoint refuses every request with 403 rather than staying open (router.py:170-188). This is a correct fail-closed design.

The config write and its audit trail are not atomic

The handler writes bilan_config_v1, then appends an audit row to bilan_config_audit_v1 as a second, independent KV write (router.py:243-250). If the audit append fails, the exception is logged and the response is still 200, because the caller’s change did take effect. If the process dies between the two writes, the config changes with no audit record. There is no transaction and no compensating action. The handler documents this trade-off explicitly; it is still a gap in a “leave an audit row behind” guarantee.

Partial updates are merged into the previous config, then re-validated as a whole (router.py:232-241). A no-op update produces no audit row. The audit trail records old and new full configs with a UTC timestamp; it does not record who called, because the token is a shared secret with no identity attached.

3.8 Cross-cutting findings

What the core layer standardises
  • One error model: BankError (500), NotFoundError (404), ConflictError (409), ValidationError (422), all surfaced as {"detail": ...} (core/errors.py:4-30; core/api.py:39-45).
  • One health path: every app built with build_app() gets an unauthenticated /health returning {"status": "ok"} (core/api.py:20-24).
  • One OpenAPI surface: the orchestrator merges every mounted sub-app into a single schema, re-prefixing paths and de-colliding operation IDs and component names (core/openapi_merge.py:1-140; orchestrator/main.py:170-177).
  • One logging shape: optional JSON lines with a service field, or a plain format that stamps the service name (core/logging.py:37-65).
Authentication is the exception, not the pattern

Outside PUT /bilan/config, no route in the engine checks a credential. Governance can ratify a canon, flip the operating mode, and clear a gate; P4 can change capital and lift a veto; P2 can approve credit—all without an authenticated actor. CORS in governance is also wide open (allow_origins=["*"] with credentials enabled) (governance/main.py:66-71). The design assumes a trusted network boundary and an external identity layer that is not in this repository. For a prudential engine, that assumption should be stated as a deployment prerequisite, not left implicit.

Carry-forward
  • One SQLite file holds both relational registries and whole-snapshot KV blobs; the KV side has no concurrency control beyond the process.
  • The persistence codec is lossless and tagged; the wire codec is flat. Keeping them separate is what makes /ready meaningful.
  • /ready proves byte-identical restart for P3 and P4 only.
  • The bilan is a pure, read-only projection; it never mutates P3/P4 and never treats a prudential buffer as cash.
  • By default the bilan is balanced by construction and honest enough to say it is not reliable.
  • The only credentialed write path is bilan config, and its audit append is not atomic with the config write.
Chapter 4 · Pillar 1

The front door that drives the whole bank: intake, lifecycle and the demo clock

Pillar 1 is defined in the governance seed as a pure data-acquisition pillar: it produces a standardised feature store and MSME data, consumes nothing, decides nothing, and must not score credit, execute loans, or set prudential policy. The implementation does all four of the things it is forbidden to do. It is not a fake pipeline, however: it calls the real Pillar 2 scoring engine, the real Pillar 3 loan engine, and the real Pillar 4 risk and staging engines in-process, then advances a deterministic monthly clock. Pillar 1 is therefore best understood as a demo orchestrator wearing the intake pillar’s name. That distinction is the key to reading every line that follows.

4.1 Canonical boundary versus implemented role

The authoritative boundary is a seed row, not prose in a README. Pillar 1 produces “Standardized feature store” and “MSME data”, consumes an empty list, decides an empty list, and carries four explicit prohibitions (governance/seed/seed.py:439-453). The demo documentation describes a much larger role: borrower intake, scoring through Pillar 2, origination and disbursement through Pillar 3, risk and IFRS 9 mirroring into Pillar 4, and a one-month tick that keeps the loan alive (project.md:235-244).

A boundary crossed by design, not by accident

This is not an implementation that secretly reimplements credit. It calls the real engines and persists their real outputs; the demo material says an imported carrier “re-runs it through the real P2 → P3 → P4 pipeline … zero fabrication” (project.md:1458-1462). The defect is architectural rather than numerical: the component that the Canon says must not decide anything is the component that sequences every decision and every money movement. Any reader who treats the governance boundary as a description of runtime authority will misread the system.

Four negative rules inside the code do survive the boundary crossing and are worth treating as real invariants:

Manual approval is never optional

MANUAL_APPROVAL_ALWAYS = True (flow.py:40). There is no automatic P1 approval path, even for an auto-approve P2 zone.

No exposure before funding

Submit scores but books no risk packet. Exposure enters P4 only at disbursement, so a submit is never blocked by a portfolio freeze (flow.py:111-114, 292-303).

No per-loan P4 interface

P1 pushes typed P3 events into P4’s inbound port; P4 never calls back into a loan (mirror.py:23-24).

No collateral valuation

P2 consumes only p4_admitted_value; P1 never values a raw asset (p2/schemas.py:81-89).

4.2 The candidate row: identity, state and the intake shape

A candidate is a row in p1_candidates, created by init_models() on the same declarative base as the governance tables, inside the shared data/orchestrator.db (p1/models.py:1-36; governance/database.py:31-41). The identifier chain is explicit and deterministic:

candidate_id → application_id (P2) → facility_id (P3) → facility_ref (P4) The P3 id is derived, not random: FAC-<candidate_id[0:10].upper()> (p2_to_p3.py:62-64).

The row is broad and deliberately flat. It stores identity, employer and salary data, the requested product and amount, a lifecycle status, five lifecycle timestamps, the P2 echo fields, and three JSON payloads: the hydrated P2 application, the deterministic repayment behaviour, and the simulator state (p1/models.py:28-103). Two computed properties are exposed: display_name and risk_rating, the latter merely returning p2_brr (models.py:105-111).

Status is free text with nine real values

status is a String(32), not an enum. The inline comment lists seven values; the code actually writes nine: new, scored, approved, rejected, disbursing, disbursed, delinquent, defaulted, and closed (models.py:65-67; service.py:208; flow.py:128, 185, 225, 282, 317; simulator.py:188, 235, 269, 276, 294). There is no database-level guard against an unknown status.

new ──submit──► scored ──approve(true)──► approved ──disburse──► disbursing ──funding──► disbursed └──approve(false)──► rejected (terminal) disbursed ──delinquency tick──► delinquent ──default tick──► defaulted ──recovery tick──► closed disbursed ──final repayment──► closed Only a successful veto check plus atomic claim reaches disbursing. rejected is terminal; closed is reached by full repayment, recovery, or a final tick.

The funding step is the only optimistic-lock path. It first checks a P4 veto, then re-scores, then runs a conditional UPDATE from approved or active to disbursing and requires exactly one affected row (flow.py:279-290; service.py:154-172). The docstring is explicit that SQLite has no FOR UPDATE, so this is the double-click and retry-on-timeout defence (service.py:157-163). active is accepted as a source state but is not written anywhere in the P1 lifecycle; it is a compatibility branch, not a real P1 state.

The rollback does not un-disburse Pillar 3

If any exception occurs after the P4 booking, the handler closes the P4 exposure best-effort and moves the candidate from disbursing back to approved (flow.py:332-344). But the P3 disburse() call at line 314 is a separate committed transition. An exception after that line—including the mirror_disburse() call at line 331—can therefore leave P3 ACTIVE while P1 is back to approved and P4 has released the exposure. There is no compensating P3 reversal on this path. The local rollback is real, but it is not distributed.

The clock is committed before the work

tick() increments and saves the global month before it processes any candidate (simulator.py:314-315). If a candidate then raises, the month is already durable, so the next tick starts from a month that was only partially applied. Default booking is deliberately retried because it uses elapsed ≥ default_month, but delinquency is one-shot (elapsed == delinquent_month, line 287). A skipped delinquency month is therefore never re-marked for a candidate that was later cured; only the default branch back-fills a missed delinquency on its way to default (simulator.py:147-151).

The tickable set is a frozenset of disbursed, delinquent and defaulted (simulator.py:28). Iteration order over a frozenset of strings depends on per-process hash randomisation, so the order candidates are processed in is not reproducible across processes, even though each candidate’s arithmetic is deterministic (simulator.py:319). The tick also catches InvalidStateTransitionError around each P3 step and compares the facility’s actual state with the expected state; if it already landed, the step becomes an idempotent no-op instead of retrying forever (simulator.py:79-96).

The background loop is best-effort: it sleeps for BANK_SIMULATION_TICK_SECONDS, calls tick(), and swallows every exception so it can never crash the task (simulator.py:340-347). The default interval is 0, which means manual ticking only; the loop starts only when the value is positive (simulator.py:353-358).

4.6 The in-process handoffs: P2 → P3 → P4

P1 never calls Pillar 2 or Pillar 3 over HTTP. It imports Pillar2Engine and constructs a module-level singleton (flow.py:11, 44). The deployable pillars/api/p2.py is a seventeen-line ASGI re-export with no P1 logic (api/p2.py:1-17). P3 and P4 are reached through their runtimes in mirror.py.

The P2-to-P3 translation is deliberately pure: it copies the packet status, maturity stage, PD-grid version and collateral track, and adds a deterministic packet id (p2_to_p3.py:32-59). It does not re-derive risk. The three human-authority fields—officer_confirmed, officer_id and human_authorised_for_workout—are supplied by P1, never inferred from the packet (p2_to_p3.py:42-46). P2’s CollateralTrack.NONE has no P3 equivalent and falls back to TRACK_1_CLASSICAL; the code explains that no pledge action is fired for an uncollateralised facility, so the field is recorded but never acted on (p2_to_p3.py:21-29).

Two deliberate accounting choices in the mirror

mirror_default() passes collateral_leg_amount = 0.0, so the whole defaulted exposure becomes the guarantee leg. The docstring explains that passing the loss as the collateral leg would make the realised net loss zero and the guarantee settlement would absorb nothing (mirror.py:117-121). mirror_disburse() must add to the given-guarantee book on every disbursement, because the pool coverage ratio is a Track 1 trigger and would otherwise stay frozen at zero (mirror.py:84-100). Both choices make the demo prudentially visible, but they also mean the simulated collateral leg is not exercised end to end.

The P4 veto is consulted exactly once per funding attempt, through p4.ports.p2.consult_veto(facility_id) (flow.py:261-271). A positive any_frozen raises a P1 state error before any booking. There is no subscription or push path: P1 reads the standing P4 state at the moment of disbursement, and a freeze that arises later has no retroactive effect on an already funded loan. The state endpoint also calls evaluate_track1_read_only(), which by design never freezes; only recompute_and_evaluate_track1() can change the standing veto (state.py:28-31).

The SSE contract is not internally consistent

The broker keeps an asyncio.Queue(maxsize=64) per subscriber and, on overflow, drops the oldest frame to make room for the newest (broker.py:13-42). The stream sends data: {json}\n\n frames and an empty heartbeat every thirty seconds, and removes the subscriber in finally (routes.py:25-42). The frame shapes then diverge: normal state updates and factory reset flatten the state into the frame root, while a tick nests it under "state" (router.py:303-305; routes.py:82, 143). A client that reads payload.state gets a value on tick and undefined on reset. DELETE publishes no frame at all (router.py:206-216), so a board watching only SSE will not notice a deletion.

Data-file reads are unconfined and one of them writes

GET /p1/demo/data-files/{filename} joins the raw path parameter onto the data directory and reads it without sanitisation (router.py:174-179). A filename such as ../../package.json resolves outside data/candidates. The listing endpoint also performs mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True) on a GET (router.py:151), so a read can create a directory. Neither issue is likely to matter inside a trusted demo laptop; both would be unacceptable at an authenticated boundary.

Factory reset is a five-step sequence: wipe the candidate table, reset the P3 engine, reset the P4 engine, re-apply the governance-approved initial Tier D so the fresh bank is not unfunded, then write the simulator clock back to zero (routes.py:130-144). It deliberately does not re-seed candidates. The project documentation lists only three steps and omits both the Tier D re-seed and the clock reset (project.md:1096-1105).

The CASS item raises only inside the dormant ABSF/CASS/restoration modules. The concentration and liquidity items raise in liquidity_concentration.py:64, 96, 102, but P1’s Track 1 consultation is a live read of P4’s veto state, not a fresh limit evaluation. The practical result is that P1’s freeze check is functional for the triggers P4 can actually evaluate—capital, EL and the G/G ratio—while concentration and liquidity remain structurally unmonitored and appear in unmonitored_triggers (state.py:37).

The missing stop is the demo constants

P1 contains no labelled governance stop, yet the thin application is full of hard-coded business assumptions: 80% drawn, MICRO, GUARANTEE, PRODUCTION, RETAIL_GENERAL, no prior default, no governance plan and two rails. These are not marked PENDING and they are not in the register. They are demo calibration values that a production intake layer would have to source from the borrower, not from code. The absence of a stop is itself a governance finding.

Carry-forward
  • P1 is the operational orchestrator of the demo, not the data-only pillar described by the Canon.
  • It calls real P2/P3/P4 engines in-process; it does not reimplement their logic.
  • Exposure enters P4 only at disbursement; that is the only point where P1 consults the P4 veto.
  • Approval asserts P4 approval to the P3 maturity gate before P4 has been asked.
  • The simulator is deterministic per candidate id, but its clock, frozenset iteration and one-shot delinquency make the sequence less reliable than the arithmetic.
  • Origination fee and risk-premium events are never mirrored, and the collateral leg is deliberately zero at default.
  • Two six-profile data sets exist; the shipped carriers are visibly stale, and the JSON set is what the orchestrator runs.
  • There are no PENDING stops in P1—only unregistered demo constants and seventeen observable defects.
Chapter 5 · Pillar 2

The credit brain: scoring, pricing and the three-zone decision

Pillar 2 is where a borrower becomes a number. It is the only pillar that assigns a Borrower Risk Rating, converts that rating into a probability of default, combines it with facility-specific loss and exposure assumptions, prices the result, and emits the packet that Pillar 3 records and executes. Unlike Pillar 1, it does not impersonate another pillar: the engine really is the Canon’s credit-scoring pillar, and its six-category scorecard is fully implemented rather than stubbed. The interesting findings are therefore not about missing arithmetic. They are about where the arithmetic stops: model-stage enforcement, collateral valuation, override feedback and the CASS contract all sit outside the engine or are represented by placeholders. Pillar 2 computes a complete credit view; it does not, by itself, enforce the governance envelope around that view.

Owns the credit estimate

Does not own loan truth

Does not own prudential truth

P2 sizes the pooled guarantee and emits a CASS shape, but P4 holds the pool, runs the veto and owns capital adequacy.

The collateral boundary is narrower than the Canon

The Canon assigns individual-case collateral admissibility and haircut to P2. The code deliberately does neither: it consumes only CollateralOffer.p4_admitted_value and applies its own LGD recovery efficiency on top (schemas.py:81-90; lgd.py:1-8, 36-60). That preserves the P2–P5 firewall and honours the P4-admissibility policy, but it means the Canon’s “P2 decides the case-level haircut” role is represented by a value P4 has already admitted. The code is internally consistent; the ownership statement is not.

Thin-file is a data-completeness flag, not a segment

is_thin_file is set when completeness is below 0.55, not when the borrower is Micro or when there is little history. A Micro borrower with a complete statement package is not thin; a Small borrower missing most fields is. That matches the feature-quality intent, but it means the thin-file route can be changed simply by filling in optional fields, even if those fields are stale.

5.5 Probability of default

PD is derived from the BRR, not from a separate model. The engine selects the versioned grid row for the assigned BRR and uses the midpoint as the point estimate; BRR 10 is the sole exception because its range is open-ended, so it uses the fixed BRR_10_PD_POINT_ESTIMATE = 0.45 (scoring.py:144-148; constants.py:54-67). The midpoint is then uplifted for data quality:

PDpoint = (PDlow + PDhigh) / 2   (or 0.45 for BRR 10)
PDMoC = min(1, PDpoint × (1 + MoCPD))
MoCPD = 0.50 × max(0, 1 − DQS)
Missing data can raise PD by at most 50% relative. A DQS of 0.50 produces a 25% uplift; a DQS of 90% produces a 5% uplift. PD is capped at 1.0 and is never reduced for good data quality.

There are no segment, product, maturity-regime or operating-model adjustments to PD. That is intentional: the Canon treats PD as a company-level estimate, while EAD and LGD carry the facility-specific treatment. The BRR grid is the only PD calibration surface in P2, and its version (v0-design) is carried into the packet so P3 and P4 can record which grid produced the number (schemas.py:181-208).

PD delivered downstream is already MoC-inclusive

The value written to packet.pd and to the P4 risk packet is pd_with_moc, not the raw grid midpoint (engine.py:82-96; p4/contracts/p2_inbound.py:58-61). P4’s contract explicitly says it must not re-apply a second buffer. The pd_range is retained only in ScoringResult; it is not sent to P3, so P3 sees the point estimate but not the calibration range.

The grid is a design anchor, not a validated PD model

BRR_PD_GRID_VERSION = "v0-design" and the file itself says the grid is provisional and must be recalibrated from the sandbox onward. No validation, back-test or population-stability check exists in P2. The point estimates are therefore policy-convenient anchors, not empirically calibrated default frequencies. The absence of a PENDING stop around the grid means the provisional status is easy to miss in the packet, which carries only the version string.

5.6 Loss given default

LGD is facility-specific and is built from the collateral P4 has already admitted. P2 never values a raw asset and has no interface with Pillar 5 (lgd.py:1-8; schemas.py:81-90). secured_recovery = Σ (p4_admitted_value × recovery_efficiencytrack)

The pooled guarantee is a separate object, not a collateral offer. Collateral class is decorative; only the track matters

Nothing in compute_lgd validates the class or uses it to vary recovery. A personal guarantee, a receivable and a real-estate pledge with the same track and admitted value all produce the same LGD. The class is carried on the input object but does not influence the number.

EAD is where product convention enters the model. PD is company-level; EAD and LGD are facility-specific (ead.py:1-7). The sandbox supports three products and deliberately refuses to score two more:

The comment says the adjustment factor is PENDING, which is honest, but the code does not raise or flag the unresolved factor. A non-recourse supply-chain facility is therefore scored exactly like a full-recourse one, with no indication in the result that a PENDING calibration was bypassed.

5.9 Decisioning and the three zones

Zone assignment is a joint test, not a BRR lookup. The Canon names three conditions: risk band, data confidence and absence of policy exceptions. The code adds a fourth auto-approve condition, that EL must be within the 2% budgeted-loss band (decisioning.py:1-41; engine.py:71-77).

5.10 Model maturity and the tier stack

Pillar 2 has a three-tier modelling stack and a three-stage model lifecycle. They are different things and should not be conflated: the tier describes which model family produced the rating; the stage describes whether that model is allowed to influence a live decision.

The stage is supplied by the caller on the application object. P2 does not query a model registry, does not compare the stage to a P4 approval, and does not reject a PRODUCTION stamp that has no approval history. The thin application builder hard-codes PRODUCTION (p1/applications.py:70-75), so the demo’s least-informed intake path receives the most mature stage by default. Enforcement is distributed across P3 and P4, but the stamp itself is unverified at the point of scoring.

5.11 Monitoring, EWS and the CASS feed

P2 produces two monitoring outputs that are often confused and are deliberately distinct. The Early-Warning Signal is facility-level, routes to a human credit officer, triggers no automatic Pillar 3 action, and is not an ABSF trigger. The CASS feed is a portfolio-level input to Pillar 4’s dormant ABSF engine (monitoring.py:1-9).

deterioration = BRRcurrent − BRRorigination

The EWS object records the origination BRR, the current BRR, the notch movement and the routing flag (monitoring.py:17-28; schemas.py:220-228). It contains no threshold calibration beyond the two-notch trigger, no persistence and no stale-data check. The two BRR values are supplied by the caller; P2 does not store the origination snapshot or compare timestamps. The False on triggers_p3_action is a hard-coded Canon invariant, not a computed risk decision.

Neither EWS nor CASS is persisted, scheduled or drift-monitored. P2 is stateless, so there is no historical BRR series, no staleness timestamp and no alert route. 5.12 Overrides and the sector overlay

The override mechanism is a learning record, not a scoring input. It captures an officer’s BRR adjustment with the model output before, the officer output after, the direction, the factor category, a reason code, a narrative and an authority level (override.py:1-57; schemas.py:231-247).

Controlled valueAllowed entries
Reason code Material information not in model; data-quality issue; qualitative risk; sector/macro overlay; collateral adjustment; model limitation; other.
Authority Senior analyst; credit manager; credit committee.
Direction DOWNGRADE if the officer BRR is numerically higher; UPGRADE if lower; UNCHANGED otherwise.

open_override validates the reason code and authority level, then returns the record. close_override completes it at maturity with the realised outcome, whether the override was vindicated, the model-improvement disposition and notes (override.py:29-75). That is a reasonable evidence loop for recalibration.

An override never changes the score, zone or packet

No engine path reads an OverrideRecord. The officer’s adjusted BRR is stored as a string and is not fed back into PD, zone assignment, pricing or the P3 packet. P1 does not call the override API. The mechanism therefore records that a human disagreed without transmitting the disagreement into the current decision. It is a learning archive, not an override.

The sector/geography overlay is a seeded lookup with a bounded blend. Sector risk and geography risk each produce a value in [0, 1]; the blended score is centred on 0.5 and scaled to at most ±1.0 (overlay.py:18-59; constants.py:148-152).

  • PD is company-level and MoC-inclusive; P4 must never apply a second buffer. EAD and LGD are facility-specific.
  • The collateral firewall holds: P2 consumes only P4-admitted value and never values a raw asset. The Canon’s P2 case-level haircut role is therefore not implemented.
  • Zone assignment is joint and ordered: high BRR, policy exceptions and thin files force manual review; auto-approve also requires DQS ≥ 0.75 and EL within 2%.

Chapter 6 · Pillar 3

Execution, ledger and lifecycle: the pillar that must not decide

Pillar 3 is the deterministic execution layer. It turns a confirmed Pillar 2 packet into a facility, moves that facility through a fixed lifecycle, captures each per-loan economic event, posts it to core banking, and writes an append-only audit record. It is also the most disciplined pillar in the codebase about what it refuses to do: the allocation guard, the PENDING registry and the explicit non-interfaces are structural enforcement, not comments. The weaknesses lie elsewhere. The execution record is thinner than the Canon requires, several lifecycle actions do not re-check the packet, the Maturity Stage gate is bypassed by the very packets P1 sends it, and the P3→P4 event route exists as an in-memory queue that never reaches P4. The boundary is well defended; the integration through that boundary is not.

6.1 Boundary: execution, not policy

The governance seed defines Pillar 3 as the deterministic loan execution layer. It produces loan-execution record entries, event-log postings, default/call event records and lifecycle deterioration detection. It consumes a confirmed P2 packet and the posting set. Its decides list is empty (orchestrator/governance/seed/seed.py:479-498).

If P1 and P3 ran as separate processes, those in-process calls would not reach the P3 service’s runtime at all. The shared global works inside one composition; it is not a production integration boundary.

The boundary with P4 is stated as a hard rule: P3 does not have a per-loan interface with P4. The code honours the negative half of that rule by refusing to emit CASS or IFRS 9 signals (p3/interfaces/non_interfaces.py:20-33). It does not implement the positive half: the documented P3→P4 event feed is a queue in memory, not a transport. That becomes the central integration finding in §6.4 and §6.7.

The absence of amounts and terms is the defining property of the model. There is no principal, interest rate, maturity date, amortisation schedule, covenant set or fee schedule on the packet. The packet says this facility may exist and this is its risk provenance; it does not contain the contract P3 is supposed to execute. is_confirmed() is therefore the only packet-level authorisation logic: an APPROVED packet is always confirmed, while PROVISIONAL and CONDITIONAL require both officer_confirmed and a non-null officer_id (packet.py:50-56). There is no signature, no issuer authentication, and no check that the packet came from P2. The API accepts the entire packet from the HTTP caller (api/p3.py:395-425), so any caller that can reach P3 can assert approval, maturity stage and officer identity.

The P2→P3 translator is pure mapping and is honest about the one lossy edge: P2 has a NONE collateral track that P3 does not, so it becomes Track 1 (p1/p2_to_p3.py:21-29, 48-59). The officer flags are not inferred from risk numbers; they are explicit arguments. In the demo P1 always passes officer_confirmed=True with a default officer id (p1/flow.py:196-201).

The loan execution record is not a loan record

The seed says P3 produces “loan execution record entries”. The code produces a stateful facility id, a packet reference, collateral objects and events. It cannot answer what the loan’s principal, rate, term or repayment obligations are. The simulator supplies those outside P3, in P1’s sim_state. P3 can execute lifecycle commands, but it cannot reconstruct or enforce the approved terms because it never receives them. That is a larger gap than any individual defect in the state machine: the authoritative per-facility record described by the Canon has no home in this build.

Two event vocabularies already disagree

P3’s posting set is LOAN_INCOME, RISK_PREMIUM_FEE, PRINCIPAL_MOVEMENT, LOSS_RECOVERY, ORIGINATION_FEE, COVENANT_BREACH and GUARANTEE_EVENT (p3/domain/enums.py:52-66). P4’s P3-inbound event enum is scheduled_repayment, origination_fee, risk_premium_accrual, default_or_write_off, recovery_collateral_leg and guarantee_call_guarantee_leg (p4/domain/events.py:33-39). Only origination_fee has even a similar name. No adapter maps one to the other. The demo avoids the collision by having P1 construct P4’s event type directly (p1/mirror.py:103-154), so the two vocabularies never meet.

Two mechanics deserve credit. The repayment and write-off guards reconstruct outstanding principal from posted events and reject any amount that would drive the balance negative, with an explicit finite-number check because NaN defeats ordinary comparisons (orchestrator.py:137-214). Default write-off is idempotent: a retried default will not write a second write-off for the facility (orchestrator.py:245-291). The state machine also refuses to let a P2 packet or P3 caller override an active veto, because the veto check runs before the packet checks (state_machine.py:62-67, 143-158; test_full_loan_lifecycle.py:99-114).

The human-authorisation rule is applied unevenly

The Canon’s rule is that P3 acts only on a confirmed, human-authorised P2 packet, and that restructuring and recovery are human decisions. The code enforces confirmation on activation, restructuring and recovery, but not on repayment, delinquency, default or closure. Those four actions accept any packet object, including an unconfirmed CONDITIONAL packet for a different facility. A caller who can reach the API can therefore mark a loan delinquent, default it, write off its principal, or close it without a confirmed decision. The state machine still blocks transitions that are structurally illegal, and it still blocks a frozen facility, but it does not block an unauthorised one.

Closing is not settlement

close() has no outstanding-balance check. A facility can be closed from ACTIVE after a single partial repayment, leaving principal unrepaid and no further lifecycle path. The simulator only closes when its own computed outstanding reaches zero (p1/simulator.py:271-275), but that invariant lives in P1, not in P3. P3’s own domain error set has guards for over-repayment and over-write-off, not for early closure.

The lifecycle is deterministic and traceable at the transition level. It is not a full loan servicing engine: there is no schedule, no accrual, no fee logic, no covenant state, no collateral release, no foreclosure action and no balance. The action set matches the states the Canon names, but not the contractual content those states are supposed to represent.

6.4 Postings and the ledger

The canonical posting set is a closed enum. P3 must not post anything outside it, and the service rejects any event type that is not a member (p3/domain/enums.py:52-66; engine/posting_service.py:60-64). The enum also declares two routing classes: events that raise an allocation question for P4, and events that are purely micro.

Posting eventRouting classProduced by the engine today

capture_and_post() is deliberately simple (posting_service.py:51-104). It validates the event type, rejects a non-numeric or non-finite amount, constructs a frozen PostingEvent, runs the allocation guard over the payload, posts to core banking, appends to the P3 event log, and — for allocation-routed events — appends an AllocationQuestion to an in-memory list. There is no branch that computes a share, a percentage or a per-tier amount. That is the strongest implementation of the corpus’s no-allocation rule in the codebase.

The routing signal is correspondingly narrow. An AllocationQuestion carries only the source event id, facility id, event type, amount and currency (posting_service.py:32-42). It contains no tier, no burden share and no allocation result. This is exactly the shape the Canon asks P3 to produce.

The allocation question never leaves Pillar 3

routed_allocation_questions() returns a copy of the in-memory list (posting_service.py:109-112). Nothing in P3 serialises or transmits that list. The P3 API exposes facilities, packets, audit, postings, ledger and reconciliation, but no route for routed questions (api/p3.py:242-392). P4 has an inbound POST /events endpoint for P3-shaped events (api/p4.py:623-640), but P3 never calls it. In the demo, P1 constructs six P4 event types itself and feeds P4 directly (p1/mirror.py:103-154). The code therefore proves that P3 can produce an allocation question without computing an allocation, but it does not implement the feed that makes the question useful. The micro/macro cut is structurally correct and operationally open.

The event log is also overloaded in a way the enum does not express. LOSS_RECOVERY is used for three economically different things: a zero-amount default marker, a positive write-off amount and a positive recovery receipt (orchestrator.py:278-307). The memo distinguishes them, and the memo strings are centralised so they do not drift (p3/domain/memos.py:1-19). But the posting type does not. Any consumer of the event log must know the memo convention to interpret the sign and meaning of the event. This is the same class of ambiguity the Four-Event Discipline is designed to prevent at P4: shared posting vocabulary across economically distinct events.

Core banking is modelled as a minimal in-memory ledger (core_banking/ledger.py:38-73). Each posting receives a sequential CB-00000001 id and is keyed by the source event id. There is one amount field and one currency field. There are no accounts, no debit/credit legs, no balance, no reversal type and no transaction grouping.

Reconciliation compares two copies of the same unsigned stream

The ledger’s total_amount() sums every posting amount as a positive number (ledger.py:72-73), and P3’s reconciliation sums its own event amounts the same way (reconciliation.py:40-42). Disbursement, repayment, write-off and recovery all add to the total. A £10,000 disbursement followed by a £500 repayment and a £9,500 write-off totals £20,000 on both sides. The comparison can only detect a missing posting, not an economically wrong one, because the model has no sign or double-entry structure to compare. The authoritative accounting system of record is therefore an append-only amount list, not a ledger.

The outstanding-principal calculation has the same limitation. It reconstructs “disbursed” and “repaid” by scanning P3’s posted PRINCIPAL_MOVEMENT events and treating every non-disbursement memo as a repayment (orchestrator.py:137-155). That is deterministic and adequate for the simulator’s linear schedule. It is not a balance engine: any future principal adjustment, reversal or partial correction with a different memo would be counted as repayment. The source of truth for the cap is P3’s own event list, not core banking, even though the Canon makes core banking the system of record.

Five of seven posting types are dead surface area

Only LOAN_INCOME, PRINCIPAL_MOVEMENT and LOSS_RECOVERY are produced by the orchestrator. There is no origination-fee capture, no risk-premium accrual, no covenant-breach record and no guarantee event. The P3 API has no route for fees, premiums or covenants. The posting set is a governance enumeration, not a description of the engine’s actual output.

6.5 Core banking and reconciliation

The P3 side of the core-banking interface is a single client class. It is the only object allowed to write the ledger; every other engine module receives the client, not the ledger (core_banking/interface.py:18-32). A static integrity test enforces that no engine or interface file outside the reconciler references CoreBankingLedger directly (tests/unit/integrity/test_governance_boundaries.py:83-109). This is a real seam, not a convention.

Reconciliation compares the sum of P3 event amounts with the ledger total and checks that every P3 event id appears among the ledger’s source event ids (reconciliation.py:35-56). The result is within tolerance only when both the amount difference and the unmatched set are clean. A second function, reconcile_or_alert(), raises on a breach so a caller cannot ignore the boolean (reconciliation.py:59-74).

The API route is explicit that it returns unmatched ids when the two sides differ (api/p3.py:377-392). Because every posting is written to the ledger synchronously by the same service call that appends it to the P3 event list, the only ordinary way to create a mismatch is to mutate the ledger externally — which the integrity test tries to prevent — or to snapshot/restore one side without the other. Reconciliation is therefore closer to an id-presence assertion than to a daily financial control. The P4-defined tolerance and the escalation path are named but not wired.

No currency, no sign, no account

CoreBankingPosting records a currency but does not validate it against the facility or against other postings. The default-marker event uses the currency string "N/A" with amount zero (orchestrator.py:278-281), which the ledger accepts. A real core-banking adapter would reject that posting or require an explicit non-monetary event type. The stand-in is intentionally simple, but the P3 event model currently depends on that simplicity: it has no way to represent a non-cash lifecycle event except as a fake financial posting.

6.6 Collateral service

P3 records two structurally distinct collateral objects and must never conflate them: the facility-specific MSME_PLEDGED asset, and the portfolio-level NEO_BANK_POOLED_GUARANTEE (p3/domain/collateral.py:19-24). The distinction is encoded as an enum, not a free-text flag, which makes conflation harder. The data object also enforces one invariant at construction: a Track 3 stablecoin object cannot be recorded under the [G] model flag (collateral.py:46-53).

The service is stricter than the packet gate in one respect and weaker in another. It independently checks packet.is_confirmed() before recording (collateral_service.py:54-59), so a conditional packet without officer sign-off cannot create collateral. But it does not consult the Maturity Stage gate or P4’s admissibility policy. It also accepts the packet’s claimed collateral track as authoritative. The choice of track is therefore only as trustworthy as the caller-supplied packet.

The P5 gate is dead code

P5Gate.record_if_confirmed() implements the Canon’s rule that a P5 object may be recorded only after admissibility confirmation has arrived through the P4→P2 chain (interfaces/p5_gate.py:21-33). No code calls it. The collateral API route records an MSME pledge from a P2 packet and a free-text external reference; it never requires a P5 object reference, an admissibility confirmation, or a check that the packet’s collateral track was actually admitted by P4 (api/p3.py:429-450). P5Gate is a correct model of the rule with no structural connection to the path that creates collateral.

The deeper gap is that P3 has no pledge lifecycle at all. Collateral is added to a facility and never released, substituted or realised. Classical foreclosure, which the Canon assigns to P2’s decision and P3’s execution/detection, is represented only by a generic RECOVERY state and a positive recovery posting (orchestrator.py:298-307). There is no foreclosure decision object, no collateral-leg amount on the event, and no linkage from the recovery posting to a specific collateral id. The P4 side has stronger typed events for the two legs; P3’s execution record does not.

6.7 The three interface gates

P3 has three inbound gates and one explicit negative interface. Together they are the most carefully reasoned part of the pillar. The problem is not their logic; it is that two of the three are not on the production call path.

GateRule implementedCalled by
P2Gate Records every packet unconditionally; authorises origination by running the Maturity Stage gate. Recording and gating are deliberately separate (interfaces/p2_gate.py:25-41). originate() only. Every other action receives the packet directly.
P4VetoRouter Applies portfolio or facility freezes; clearing requires an explicit P4 call; the state machine refuses all transitions while frozen (interfaces/p4_router.py:31-73). No production caller. Only tests call apply_*_veto(); the API exposes no route.
P5Gate Returns a P5 object reference only if the packet carries a confirmed P4→P2 admissibility chain (interfaces/p5_gate.py:21-33). No caller.

The P2 gate’s split between recording and authorising is correct and well documented. A packet is useful evidence even when it cannot yet authorise money. But the gate is only consulted at origination. disburse() calls state_machine.activate(), which checks confirmation but not the Maturity Stage gate; pledge_collateral() checks confirmation directly; repayment, delinquency, default and closure do not check confirmation at all. The API’s disburse, pledge, repayment and lifecycle routes each call p2_gate.receive_packet() before the action (api/p3.py:422-577), which updates the packet record but does not run the authorisation gate.

The P4 veto router is a test-only control

The actual P4 veto in the demo is enforced in P1 at disbursement, by querying P4’s consult_veto() before booking exposure (p1/flow.py:261-271). P3’s own P4VetoRouter is never fed by P4. Its apply_portfolio_veto() and apply_facility_veto() methods are called only in tests. As a result, veto_status() — the value written into every audit record as p4_veto_status — reports CLEAR on the production path even when P4 has frozen the portfolio. The API description says a P4 freeze “surfaces here as a 409” (api/p3.py:395-405), but no P3 route consults P4. The freeze is real; P3’s representation of it is not.

This is a boundary success and an integration failure at the same time. The state machine cannot be overridden by P2 or P3 once a veto flag is set (state_machine.py:62-67; test_full_loan_lifecycle.py:99-114), which is exactly what the Canon demands. But the only way to set the flag in this build is a test call. A production P4 freeze does stop disbursement — because P1 checks P4 before calling P3 — while P3 continues to believe, and to audit, that the facility is clear.

The non-interface module is a model of governance engineering

emit_cass_feed() and emit_ifrs9_staging_signal() do nothing except raise a GovernanceBoundaryError quoting the interface rule (non_interfaces.py:20-33). They are not wired to any caller. Their value is that a future engineer who tries to forward a staging signal from P3 — a superficially reasonable idea, since P3 sees the delinquency event — hits an explicit refusal instead of silently creating an interface the Canon forbids. This is the PENDING/boundary discipline applied correctly: make the wrong action impossible, not merely undocumented.

6.8 Allocation guard and maturity gate

The allocation guard is the clearest expression of the Canon’s structural rule. The module contains no allocation function at all. It exposes one assertion that fails if a payload carries any key resembling a per-tier split, burden share, ABSF amount or loss repartition (governance/allocation_guard.py:26-50). The posting service runs that assertion on every event before posting it (posting_service.py:85-89).

The stronger enforcement is the test suite. One test parses every P3 source file and fails if any forbidden allocation identifier appears outside the guard itself (tests/unit/integrity/test_governance_boundaries.py:47-59). Another drives a full lifecycle and asserts that no posted event or routed question carries a tier field (test_governance_boundaries.py:112-148). This is exactly the right shape: a runtime check for the ordinary path, a static check for future code, and an end-to-end check that the two agree. P3 does not merely promise not to allocate; it makes allocation logic detectable if someone adds it.

The Maturity Stage gate is a different story. Its documented rule is unambiguous (governance/maturity_gate.py:1-15):

StageDocumented ruleActual gate behaviour
DESIGN No live loans; [G] built, [S] stubbed. No origination path at all. If is_confirmed() is true, the gate returns before it looks at the stage. A confirmed DESIGN packet can originate.
SANDBOX Live pilot loans; a human credit-officer gate is mandatory; no auto-origination path may exist. An unconfirmed SANDBOX packet is refused. A confirmed one passes, which is correct for the human gate but does not distinguish DESIGN from SANDBOX.
PRODUCTION Conditional auto-consumption only after explicit P4 approval and Orchestrator notification. An unconfirmed packet requires a valid ProductionAutoApproval object. The object is caller-constructed and carries no proof.

The critical line is the first check: if packet.is_confirmed(): return (maturity_gate.py:43-48). The comment justifies it as “the human gate has already been satisfied”, which is true for a conditional packet that an officer has counter-signed. But is_confirmed() also returns true for every APPROVED packet regardless of maturity stage (domain/packet.py:50-56). P2 can emit APPROVED in DESIGN, and P1 translates every scored result with officer_confirmed=True (p1/flow.py:196-201). The result is that the demo’s packets never exercise the maturity gate at all: they are confirmed, and the gate’s first branch exits immediately.

The P1 production approval is both fabricated and unused

At approval P1 constructs ProductionAutoApproval(approved_by_p4=True, orchestrator_notified=True) without asking P4 or the Orchestrator (p1/flow.py:203-213). The object is a two-boolean dataclass whose is_valid() is simply an and (maturity_gate.py:26-35). Any caller can create a valid one. Because the translated packet is APPROVED, the gate returns before it inspects this object, so the fabricated approval has no effect. If the packet were instead an unconfirmed PRODUCTION packet, the same object would authorise auto-origination. The gate therefore manages to be simultaneously bypassed on the normal path and forgeable on the path where it matters.

P4 does implement a real model-maturity registry. It has separate approve_sandbox_graduation() and approve_production_graduation() operations, and the production transition is explicitly not automatic (p4/domain/model_maturity.py:38-88). P3 never calls it. The maturity stage on the packet is taken from P2, which in turn takes it from its caller (p2/schemas.py:109; p2/engine.py:78-95). There is no registry lookup, no approval-id check and no Orchestrator notification anywhere on the P3 path. The governance machinery exists in P4 and is disconnected from the gate that is supposed to consume it.

The gate can also be skipped entirely. originate() is the only method that calls authorize_origination() (orchestrator.py:106-116). A caller can call disburse() on an id that was never originated: the method creates the facility on demand and the state machine only requires a confirmed packet (orchestrator.py:118-127; state_machine.py:113-121). A confirmed DESIGN packet therefore reaches ACTIVE without ever touching the maturity gate. pledge_collateral() similarly checks only is_confirmed(). The gate is a check on one entry point, not an invariant on the facility.

The PENDING registry, by contrast, is well built. Five items are registered with their owning register and the P3 capability they block. Each is exposed only through stub(), which always raises; there is deliberately no function that returns a resolving value (governance/pending_registry.py:24-88). An integrity test asserts that the module’s entire public function set is {stub, is_registered} and that every item is unresolvable (test_governance_boundaries.py:62-80).

6.9 Audit trail

The audit model is the strongest data contract in P3. AuditRecord makes every field the Spec names a required attribute, not an optional one (p3/domain/events.py:41-65). A record that is missing the packet version, maturity stage, grid version, veto status, core-banking confirmation or resulting state cannot be constructed at all.

AuditTrail is append-only at the type level: it exposes no update or delete method, stores records in a list, and returns a shallow copy from all_records() (audit/trail.py:20-36). The records themselves are frozen dataclasses. An integrity test drives a run and asserts that the trail only grows and earlier records remain at their original positions (test_governance_boundaries.py:151-170). Within one process, the append-only property holds.

Append-only is not the same as retention

The audit trail lives in the same snapshot as everything else. The P3 runtime serialises audit_trail._records into SQLite and rehydrates it on restart (api/p3.py:136-138, 158-162). The demo’s factory reset replaces the entire orchestrator with a pristine one and persists an empty snapshot (api/p3.py:181-188); P1 exposes that reset as a normal HTTP route (p1/routes.py:120-144). A single API call therefore erases every audit record, facility, packet and ledger posting. There is no seven-year retention rule, no immutable store, no tombstone and no reset audit record. The trail is append-only while the process lives; it is deletable by design.

The trail records successes, not decisions

Audit records are written only after an action succeeds. A refused Maturity Stage gate, a rejected unconfirmed packet, a vetoed transition, a PENDING stop, an over-repayment and a reconciliation breach all raise before _record_audit() is called. The trail therefore cannot answer whether an unsafe action was attempted and refused. It also records no veto application or clearance, because the router has no audit hook. The Canon’s question — which packet version, which maturity stage and what veto status were in effect — is answerable for successful actions only, and the veto answer is wrong whenever P4 is the one freezing the book.

The orchestrator’s docstring says every public method ends by appending exactly one audit record (orchestrator.py:1-9). The code is more honest than the comment: a repayment appends two records, one for interest and one for principal (orchestrator.py:236-237), and a default with a write-off appends two, one for the marker and one for the write-off (orchestrator.py:282-290). That is the correct granularity for a posting-level trail, but it means “one action, one record” is not a safe assumption for a consumer.

The larger audit weakness is correlation. Records carry a facility id and a packet version, but not the packet id, the application id, the P4 approval id or a correlation id for the request that caused the action. Two facilities can share a packet version; a packet id is the only stable link back to the P2 decision. The audit API can list all records or filter by facility (api/p3.py:315-340), but it cannot reconstruct the full causal chain from one P2 decision through origination, disbursement, servicing and workout.

6.10 Verified defects and boundary leaks

P3 is the most disciplined pillar on paper and the least complete in production wiring. The boundary rules are real: no allocation logic, no staging emission, no pooled-guarantee accounting, no policy decision. The defects are concentrated in authorisation coverage, event transport, accounting depth and persistence. They are implementation gaps, not governance ambiguities, so none of them is legitimately PENDING.

Three boundary rules survive inspection and are worth naming as successes, because they are the parts a P4 build can rely on:

No allocation logic

Guarded at runtime, scanned statically and asserted end-to-end. P3 produces a question, never a share.

No staging interface

CASS and IFRS 9 emission raise explicitly. The P2→P4 direct feed cannot be accidentally routed through P3.

No pooled-guarantee accounting

The two collateral types are separate enum values and the pooled side is a PENDING stop, not a guessed implementation.

Two further boundary leaks originate outside P3 but land in it. P1 drives the real demo by calling P3’s global runtime in-process rather than through the API (p1/mirror.py:13-15; p1/flow.py:354-356), and it feeds P4 its own hand-built events while P3’s allocation questions accumulate unread (p1/mirror.py:103-154). The HTTP surface exists and is documented, but the governance-relevant path bypasses it. P3’s dialect is correct; the live system is speaking a different one.

What this chapter carries into Pillar 4

P4 cannot assume that P3 has enforced a maturity stage, a P4 veto or a P5 admissibility check. Those are either bypassable or dead. P4 must treat the packet’s stage and confirmation as claims, not proofs, until the P2/P4 approval chain is actually wired. P4 also cannot assume a usable P3 event feed: the vocabulary differs, no adapter exists and the only live bridge is P1’s mirror. The no-allocation rule, by contrast, is trustworthy: P4 can consume P3 events without re-checking for embedded shares, provided it builds the missing transport. Finally, the core-banking ledger is a stub. P4’s reconciliation tolerance and its “core banking prevails” rule have nothing real to bind to yet.

The business reading is unflattering but clear. P3 is a well-governed execution skeleton with a strong negative-space design and a thin, non-authoritative accounting core. It can take a confirmed packet and walk a facility through a plausible lifecycle, and it will refuse the things the Canon forbids. It cannot yet prove that the packet was legitimate, take an unscheduled real-world loan through its actual terms, record collateral beyond a reference, or hand its economic events to the pillar that is supposed to allocate them. Those are the build items that matter before any live pledge.

Chapter 7

Pillar 4 — prudential and guarantee accounting

Part I (core prudential mechanics) and Part II (guarantee-pool accounting and the buffer), with a deliberately dormant adaptive layer and the SI-1 mode gate.

7.1 Two halves and a dormant third

Pillar 4 owns the prudential nervous system: it never decides who gets a loan (P2) and never executes loans (P3). It owns whether the whole balance sheet stays safe. The canon splits it into two functional halves, both always-on in Conventional Mode:

  • Part I — Core Prudential Mechanics: tier consumption, the two veto tracks, IFRS 9/provisioning, bail-in (framework only), liquidity and concentration limits, core-banking governance (claude.md §1).
  • Part II — Guarantee-Pool Accounting and the Buffer: the buffer account with four sub-accounts, event routing from P3, guarantee-call settlement, guarantee-pool custody and adequacy, pledge-release management (claude.md §1).

A third dimension is the operating mode: Conventional Mode (Stage 1, complete and licensable) vs Adaptive Mode (Stage 2, adds CASS, ABSF, Reverse Restoration Waterfall, Tier B-Fees interruption/resumption). Everything adaptive is specified but dormant; deactivation is not deletion. The Orchestrator owns the tier table, veto-resolution point, CASS contract shape and mode allocation; Pillar 4 consumes them with no carve-out.

The micro/macro cut is absolute: P3 produces per-loan events with no allocation logic; P4 aggregates, allocates and computes capital impact. Pillar 4 has no per-loan interface with P3 — only a confirmed/withdrawn P2 packet reaches P3. Staging signals go P2→P4 directly (bypassing P3). (claude.md §2)

7.9 Part II: guarantee-call settlement

Settlement sequence: (1) draw on called pool = realized loss net of collateral leg, (2) passage through reserve/provision sub-accounts (EL Pool, then ULA Buffer beyond Budgeted Loss), (3) distribution to funders (Senior Institutional Debt, Tier C, Tier D). Only P4 computes/settles; P2 sizes; P3 only records (no allocation). Code paths/vocabularies must never be shared with pledge release or classical foreclosure.

7.10 Part II: pool custody and pledge release

guarantee_pool_service.py holds pool composition (Tier A + Tier B), receives required level from P2, monitors adequacy, enforces admissibility policy (eligible types, haircuts, concentration), and maintains given/received books that back the leverage ratio. P4 never re-sizes the required level; it only compares. Stage 1 is Track 1 (classical) collateral only (Tracks 2/3 gated).

pledge_release.py enforces the release invariant: no cash movement, no LR draw. Release is requested at/post-term (with notice window) or by exception pre-term with penalty. During the window, adequacy is managed via origination pacing, replacement pledge intake, or Track 1 veto if shortfall coincides with a ratio breach. Release is a right-liberation of RWA, not redemption.

adaptive_dormant/ contains cass_engine.py, absf.py, restoration_waterfall.py as inert modules (built, not activated). Sub-account sockets are reserved. No CASS methodology/weights/thresholds appear in Part II; full P2+P4 CASS spec is a named PENDING item blocking Stage 2.

7.12 The SI-1 mode gate

mode_gate/mode_gate.py and domain/mode_state.py implement the governance-controlled switch. Transitions Conventional ↔ Adaptive require a governance ruling; fallback requires no rebuild, no book unwind, no contract amendment; Conventional Mode is the permanent floor. Adaptive components are deactivated, not removed.

Chapter 8

End-to-end flows

8.1 Origination

Canonical: P2 scores/decides (BRR/PD/LGD/EAD/EL). P2 checks against P4 policy/thresholds. P4 veto consulted at confirmation; if frozen, packet withheld/withdrawn (resolves at P2). P3 only acts on confirmed packet. P1 translates P2 result to P3 packet (sets officer_confirmed=True), calls P3 in-process, and checks P4 veto at disbursement. Collateral recorded only if confirmed and Track 1/MSME (Tracks 2/3 gated). (p1/flow.py, p3/governance/maturity_gate.py, p4/part1_core_prudential/veto_engine.py)

8.2 Live servicing

P3 emits events (repayment splits interest/principal into postings; delinquency transitions). Events route via Event Router to buffer sub-accounts (P4). P4 recomputes capital from balances; staging signals come P2→P4 directly. Allocation questions are produced but not transmitted in production path.

8.3 Deterioration and default

Default/write-off/recovery use LOSS_RECOVERY with distinct memos. Collateral leg (foreclosure) decided by P2, detected/executed context via P3; guarantee leg computed/settled by P4 (draw pool, route through EL/ULA, distribute to funders). P3 records events; no allocation logic in P3. P4 cannot rely on P3 veto/maturity enforcement (see §7.13).

8.4 The prudential veto

Track 1 is standing (freezes action class until governance lifts). Track 2 is facility-level. Notification to Orchestrator mandatory. Resolution at P2 only. In current build, P4 veto router not wired into P3 API; P1 enforces by querying P4 before disbursement.

8.5 Pledge intake and release

P5 submits pledge → P4 checks admissibility policy (P4 does not decide case). P2 confirms individual case. P4 records inflow, updates pool adequacy. Release: notice window, adequacy managed via pacing/replacement/intake or Track 1 veto; invariant no cash, no LR draw.

8.6 Mode activation and fallback

Conventional ↔ Adaptive via governance ruling (SI-1). Fallback requires no rebuild, no book unwind, no contract amendment; Conventional is permanent floor; adaptive components deactivated not removed.

8.7 The demo tick and reset

P1 drives simulation (originate/pledge at approval, disburse after P4 veto check, drives lifecycle). POST /api/p1/reset replaces P3 and P4 runtimes and persists empty snapshot — erases audit/ledger/facilities.

Chapter 11

Conclusions

P3 is a well-governed execution skeleton with strong negative-space design (no allocation, no staging emission, no pooled guarantee). It can run a plausible lifecycle on confirmed packets but cannot prove legitimacy, cannot enforce maturity/veto/P5 fully, cannot represent loan terms/collateral lifecycle, and cannot hand events to P4 via a working adapter. P4 correctly separates Part I and II, enforces veto boundaries, maintains the four-sub-account buffer with dormant adaptive sockets, and refuses PENDING items. The main integration gaps are transport (P3→P4 events), enforcement wiring (veto/maturity), accounting depth, and API hardening. Conventional Mode is coherent as a standalone licensable bank; Stage 2 remains gated by governance and the missing CASS spec.
